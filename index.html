Indie Games · Eldritch House

Shadow Sacrament: The Roots of Evil

Embedded researcher for a Brazil-based indie studio's survival horror game. Moderated playtests and a frame-by-frame PT-BR localization audit, with one finding escalated and reviewed by the dev team the same day it was delivered.

Role
Embedded UX Researcher: owned the research end to end, from plan to readout, including 7 live-moderated sessions, a localization audit, and cross-session synthesis
Context
Embedded research partner to a Brazil-based indie studio
The game
Shadow Sacrament: The Roots of Evil is a 2D survival horror game in the style of classic Resident Evil, made by Eldritch House, a small studio in Brazil. It has five acts. Testers get each act as a separate build on Steam.
The question
When players got stuck, was it because the puzzles were hard, or because the game was not telling them what to do?
My role
The studio's only researcher. I ran the study from plan to readout: 25 playtesters, 19 sessions (7 moderated live), 23 written reports.
What changed
The developers fixed 3 issues I reported: a missing room on the Act 3 map, a collider that trapped a player in an Act 2 cave, and a Steam launch error. Three more confirmed bugs went to them before release.

1The problem

The studio was building toward release with a small team and builds that changed act by act. They approved a two-week research plan built on four questions:

  • Do players understand what a puzzle wants from them?
  • Do they notice the game's feedback when they act?
  • Can they use the map to know where they are and where to go?
  • Does the map feel like part of the game's world?

In scope: puzzles, menus such as inventory and files, and the map. Out of scope: combat balance, boss tuning, difficulty and story. Players raised those anyway. I recorded their comments and passed them on when the developers' per-act questions asked for them, but I did not study them. The localization audit below was added later and was not in the original plan.

2How I ran it

I recruited and screened playtesters in several countries, including volunteers from the game's Discord server. A session meant one person playing one act and giving feedback on it. I call them playtests because that is the studio's word, but the method is closer to a small, longer usability study: I was watching for where players hesitated or misread something, not measuring how much they enjoyed the game. There were two formats:

  • Moderated, live (7 sessions). I watched on a call while the player thought aloud, then asked short follow-up questions while they still remembered what confused them.
  • Unmoderated (the other 12). Players played on their own, then sent written feedback, usually with screen recordings or phone clips.

Because each act is a separate build, I had to decide which act each tester played. I counted how often they died outside boss fights, as a rough signal of comfort with the game, and sent more comfortable players to later acts. The weakness is that deaths can come from confusing feedback as well as low skill. One tester lost most of his runs to axe parry timing, and some enemies showed no stealth indicator, so I would not use this signal alone again.

Each session also had a survey component, using the developers' standard per-act questions. They were open-ended and answered in writing by a few players per act, so they gave me quotes and patterns, not numbers. Every report covered the same things: what the player liked most and least, what confused them, their suggestions, and whether the sneak, map, stamina and crafting systems were clear. Each report carries the build number, which the developers later asked for in all reports.

3What players struggled with: puzzles and feedback

Feedback was the weaker side of the game, more than puzzle difficulty.

  • Pause menu. The cursor started on Load Game, a destructive action, instead of Resume Game.
  • Stealth. Some enemies showed no stealth indicator, and players got little signal when they were spotted.
  • Silent responses. In Act 3, an item used at the next spot gave no visual change, sound or response, so players could not tell whether they had the right place or the right item.
  • Teaching gap. 12 minutes passed between picking up the lantern and learning how to activate it.
  • What worked. Players singled out the Carbine file puzzle and the tap-to-enter safe codes.

One tester skipped story content in Acts 3 to 5 because doors and key items were not gated in that build. He put it down to the build being unfinished.

4What players struggled with: the map

  • Two players got lost in Act 3. The map did not show the Exit Door puzzle room, which the developers confirmed as a known issue.
  • A later tester found the map clear overall but harder to read in Acts 4 and 5, where fewer landmarks gave him less to orient by.
  • I have the least evidence on the fourth question, whether the map feels like part of the world, so I do not claim an answer to it.

5A second job: checking the Portuguese version

The game ships in English and Brazilian Portuguese (PT-BR). Partway through, I recorded a 94-minute PT-BR playthrough and reviewed more than 1,100 frames from it, looking for text in the wrong language or cut off. That became 7 localization findings, each with a timestamp and steps to reproduce. One of them, PT-BR text appearing in the English interface, was a confirmed bug. Reviewing recordings frame by frame is also how the pause menu cursor finding surfaced.

6What happened next

  • The Act 3 map now shows the missing room, with a [?] marker for unfinished business.
  • The latest build's pause menu starts on Resume Game. The developers could not say whether my report prompted it, but agreed that Load Game as the first option invites accidental presses.
  • The Act 2 cave collider and the Steam launch error were fixed within two days of the player's report. Each report went to the developers with the player's recordings and the build number.
  • Three more bugs I confirmed went to them: PT-BR text in the English UI, keybind remapping that decoupled animation from door function, and reversed enemy audio spatialization.
  • The developers called the missing response to an item in Act 3 fair feedback. They also said the safe combination, statue and lock puzzles can be solved from the in-game notes and have brute-force fallbacks so players cannot get locked out. That leaves one open question: whether players find and read those notes.

7What I would do differently

  • Add a short self-reported experience question to the death-count routing, so it does not end up sorting players by how much the game confused them.
  • Apply the recurring-findings rule earlier: flag an issue once, and if the team does not respond, move it to an appendix so the priority signal stays clear across acts.
Reflection

Working with a small studio taught me that the delivery of a finding matters as much as the finding itself. Framing research as serving the game, before testing even begins, and acknowledging design intent before describing where it broke down for players kept the relationship collaborative rather than adversarial, even when the news wasn't good.

Self-Initiated Study · Clockwork Acorn

Feather's Edge: New Player Controls Study

A volunteer study I proposed to a small independent studio: how new players learn an unusual movement and combat scheme in the October 2026 demo. Seven of 11 respondents said the jump did not behave as expected, even though 9 of 11 rated the controls clear.

Role
Independent researcher: study scoping, survey design, recruitment, analysis, and findings report
Context
Volunteer study I proposed to a small independent studio, run on the October 2026 demo of their upcoming platform game
The game
Feather's Edge is a platform game from Clockwork Acorn, a small independent studio. Its movement and combat scheme differs from most platformers, and the studio prefers to teach the controls briefly and let players learn through play.
The question
Can new players work out that scheme on their own, what surprises them along the way, and where do they give up?
My role
Independent researcher. I proposed the study, then scoped it, designed the survey, recruited players, analyzed the answers and wrote the report.
What this can and cannot tell you
The survey closed at 11 respondents against a target of 15, all self-selected from online communities. That is too few to say how common any problem is, so I report counts, not percentages or averages. Read the findings as leads to check against the studio's larger in-game survey.

1How the study came about

In May 2026 I offered Clockwork Acorn free research. After a call with the studio's head in September, we agreed on a one-off study of how new players learn the controls in the public demo (the October 2 build), with participation capped and players recruited from my own channels. The studio's own in-game survey already reached a wider audience, so mine was built to explain why patterns happen, not to measure how often.

In scope: the first-time experience of that demo: controls, movement and combat rules. Out of scope: the input scheme itself, which the studio had locked, and any other build.

2How I ran it

  • Survey design. I wrote a survey for the studio to review before launch. They cleared it as revealing nothing about unreleased content. Closed questions gave counts. Open questions, such as "What did you learn about fighting that the game never told you?", captured the rules players found on their own and the words they used for what surprised them.
  • Recruiting. I posted the game's trailer in Reddit communities for indie and Metroidvania-style games and in some Discord channels. The studio's own community was small and had already been asked to complete the in-game survey, so we agreed I would find my own players. I stopped engaging in the studio's Discord when asked.
  • Result. 11 responses against a target of 15.

3What players told me

Movement. 9 of 11 rated the controls clear in the first few minutes, yet 7 said the jump did not behave as they expected. They knew which button did what. The movement itself surprised them. Only 4 said movement felt natural by the end of the first level, 5 said later in the demo and 2 said never. Three players independently asked for a way to cancel a jump or release from a wall.

Rules they found on their own. 4 discovered that dashing makes them immune to projectiles. 6 described a rule tied to a single enemy type. Two players never worked out how to defeat one enemy and stopped playing there. 6 said they knew why they took damage only some of the time, and nobody chose never.

Where they stopped. 2 finished the demo, 5 said they stopped early and 4 left the question blank. 3 of the 5 who stopped did so in the ice region. Three players also hit input problems on three different non-Xbox controllers. Only Xbox controllers are officially supported, so some of those may be known limits.

4What I would and would not conclude

  • Worth following up: the gap between "the controls are clear" and "the jump surprised me", the cancel and wall-release requests, and the ice region as a place players quit.
  • Not safe to conclude: how many players overall share any of these problems. With 11 self-selected respondents, one or two people change a result.
  • Weak spots in my data: the question set changed between the first 5 responses and the last 6, so several counts are out of 5 or 6, not 11. And 9 of 11 knew the genre well, so the study says little about players new to platformers.

5What happened next

The findings report went to the studio on October 19. The studio reviewed it and discussed it as a team. Their reply was that they had already identified almost all of it, so it gave them no new information to act on. That means this study did not change their plans. It also means the survey reached, on its own, problems the studio already knew about, which is some evidence that it pointed at real issues. I cannot claim an effect on the game.

6What I would do differently

  • Ask the studio at the start which problems it already knows about, so the study targets what it does not.
  • Ask every respondent the same questions, so every count has the same base.
  • Recruit players new to platformers, not only genre veterans.
  • Aim for a larger sample, or pair the survey with a few observed sessions, so patterns do not rest on one or two answers.
Reflection

A self-selected sample of 11 cannot say how common a problem is. It can show what the problems look like and what players say about them, which is why the report gives counts instead of percentages and lists its limits. The studio's larger in-game survey can show whether the jump, dash, and ice region patterns hold at scale.

Role
Apprentice in Phase 1, then Co-Lead UX Researcher in Phase 2 (Mar to May 2023): one of 3 co-leads on an 8-person research team
Team Context
Volunteer research team via Tech Fleet, working alongside the design, content, and product strategy teams
The project
Kadena K-Block Explorer V2 was a Tech Fleet volunteer project to build a second version of Kadena's blockchain explorer with three goals: an easy-mode interface, analytics for Kadena NFTs, and real-time trading tools. A blockchain explorer is a site where people look up transactions, tokens and wallets.
The question
The first prototype rested on two personas and only two exploratory interviews. Could first-hand evidence from crypto and NFT users update those personas and show what to fix in the prototype?
My role
Apprentice UX researcher in Phase 1 (October 2022 to March 2023), then one of 3 co-leads of an 8-person research team in Phase 2 (March to May 2023).
What this can and cannot tell you
The interview and usability numbers are team results, and each usability round had only a few participants. They show where people struggled, not how often. I was a co-lead, not the sole researcher, and Phase 3 was run by a different team.

1The problem

Phase 1 produced a competitive analysis, two exploratory interviews and personas, but the design team's first prototype was built around two personas, a rookie user and a returning user, with only those two interviews behind them. Web3 users are also wary of scams, which makes recruiting hard. Phase 2 had eight weekly sprints, from March 20 to May 12, 2023, to get first-hand evidence from crypto and NFT users and to test the prototype.

2What I did in Phase 1

As an apprentice, I contributed to two competitive analyses (block explorers and trading analytics tools). I created a recruitment survey and flyer, and a chart of platforms for recruiting users of all levels. I ran in-depth interviews with mid-level users when time and material were limited, ran a Figma brainstorming session with all teams, and helped produce the affinity map and personas.

3How Phase 2 ran

I stepped up to co-lead. The research team recruited participants, ran interviews and usability sessions, and passed findings to the design team in small batches while the work continued.

  • Methods. A competitive analysis of NFT analytics tools, to find where the explorer could fill a gap. Nine interviews, 6 with participants experienced in crypto and NFT trading and 3 with current Kadena users, to update the personas. Two rounds of moderated usability testing, first on the Blockchain Explorer and then on the NFT Analytics Tool, using tasks the design team handed off. Transcripts were coded in FigJam, and each interviewer and coder wrote a short proto-persona so the team could build personas together.
  • Recruiting. Some people who shared their contact details later backed out. The team reached out to active Kadena users on Twitter and Discord and through friends and family, starting with a goal of 5 interviews. Tech Fleet's CXO advised that even one participant a week was valuable. Interviews grew from 2 to 9.
  • Out of scope. New-to-crypto users, who were not recruited until Phase 3.

My part: I presented the NFT analytics competitive analysis at the Sprint 1 kickoff, contributed usability task ideas ahead of the joint task session with the design team, and contributed to recruiting through direct outreach. As one of 3 co-leads, I shared responsibility for the team's deliverables at the May 2023 Final Sprint Demo.

4What we found

  • Interviews. Participants preferred mainstream explorers like Etherscan to verify tokens and transactions, used tools like CoinGecko for price data, and relied on social media for the information behind their decisions. Their reasons for using Kadena were smart contracts, low gas fees and community visibility.
  • Round 1 (Blockchain Explorer). All 3 users completed every task step except one, which 2 of 3 completed. The dropdown menu went unused and mining terms confused people, while the familiar layout drew praise.
  • Round 2 (NFT Analytics Tool). The Single Ease Question score was 6.7 of 7, a team result, and finding the NFT calendar was the main struggle.

5What happened next

Later Tech Fleet teams carried the work through Phases 3 and 4, and I was not on those teams. Phase 3 opened by reanalyzing the Phase 1 and 2 data to set its research goals. Its design team started from the Phase 2 prototype, ran usability tests on it, and rebuilt pages including the NFT dashboard, neighboring chains and wallet overview. Phase 2 had reached mostly experienced users, and Phase 3 recruited Newbies alongside two experience levels. Phase 3 also ran its own usability study and added a Resources page with a glossary and tooltips after its own testing found that blockchain terms confused many participants. That matches the mining-term confusion we saw in Phase 2 Round 1. By the end of Phase 3 the design team had a beta-launch-ready responsive web app handed off to developers, with a live site shown at the September 2023 demo. The front-end beta is publicly hosted at kblock-explorer.vercel.app; it still shows placeholder data because Phase 3 recommended that the next phase focus on backend development. Phase 3's research team recommended updating personas and testing updated screens next, and Phase 4 interviewed two beginner groups.

6What I would do differently

  • Separate interviews and usability testing by default. We combined them in one session, which sounds efficient but can be too much for a participant with a tight schedule. Separate sessions suit those participants, and a combined session fits someone with a wide-open schedule. The team's retrospective also found that the combined format cut into the depth of the interviews.
  • Have every team send members to other teams' meetings, since that kept the projects aligned.
Reflection

I started this project doubting it. Several crypto companies closed while we worked, and researching a crypto product felt risky. Finding active communities across social media changed that: people were still busy with cryptocurrency, mining, and NFTs, and many were willing to talk with us and suggest improvements. Sitting in on other teams' meetings also paid off. Knowing what each team needed let us build parts of their tasks into our research, and it gave us direction when our own next steps were unclear. Completing two rounds of interviews and usability testing in eight weeks was a real accomplishment for the team.

Disney Speedstorm UX Research Study

Independent observational study on a live-service mobile racing game. Five unmoderated think-aloud sessions across three countries surfaced a recurring confusion around currency and reward systems.

Role
Independent researcher: study design, audience targeting, and cross-session synthesis
Context
Independent observational study conducted via PlaytestCloud credits received upon completing Steve Bromley's Games User Research Fundamentals course
The game
Disney Speedstorm is a free-to-play kart racing game for mobile, made by Gameloft, with a complex progression system of currencies, shards and passes that new players meet from the first session.
The question
Do new players understand what the in-game currencies and rewards are for, and does the early game teach them what to do next?
My role
Independent researcher. No studio was involved. I designed the study, chose the participants and synthesized the sessions.
What this can and cannot tell you
Five players is enough to see what confusion looks like and where it repeats. It is not enough to say how common it is, so I report counts and treat the results as patterns worth testing further.

1Why I ran this study

I finished Steve Bromley's Games User Research Fundamentals course and received PlaytestCloud credits for completing it. I used them on a real game as a practical exercise. Because no studio commissioned the work, the question was mine, written as if for the game's product team.

In scope: new player onboarding, progression comprehension and early-game friction. Out of scope: anything that needed studio access or internal data, such as analytics or balance numbers.

2How I ran it

Five new players recorded themselves playing and talking through what they were thinking, using PlaytestCloud. This is an unmoderated think-aloud method, so I could not ask follow-up questions in the moment. The players were aged 17 to 36 and lived in the US, Canada and the UK. That gave about 3.5 hours of footage. PlaytestCloud's AI-generated session summaries and annotation tags were my main data source, and I looked for patterns that recurred across sessions.

3What players struggled with

  • Currencies and rewards. 4 of 5 independently said they were confused about the in-game currencies, shards and reward systems, regardless of age or gaming experience. This was the most consistent finding.
  • Onboarding split by player type. Experienced kart-racing players found it sufficient. Players new to the genre were still "winging it" after 30 minutes.
  • Special skill. 2 of 5 struggled to understand it, even though the tutorial covered it.
  • Less Disney than expected. 2 felt the experience was not distinctively Disney.

4What worked

  • All 5 praised the responsive controls and the visuals.
  • Character upgrades, ranking rewards and the Golden Pass were named as strong motivators once players understood them.

5What I would and would not conclude

  • Worth testing further: whether the currency confusion holds with more players, and whether new-to-genre players really struggle more than experienced ones.
  • Not safe to conclude: how many players overall share the confusion. Four of five players in a small group can change with one or two different recruits.
  • Weak spot in my sample: 4 of 5 played 14 or more hours a week, and I did not recruit players who came to the game for Disney, so the experience of that audience is a gap.

6What happened next

The study produced a synthesis report with 7 prioritized findings and 5 design recommendations, linked below. No studio was involved, so the report is where the work ends, and I cannot say whether anything changed.

7What I would do differently

  • Recruit specifically for players who came for Disney, to understand the experience gap for that audience.
  • Run a second round to test whether specific changes fix the currency confusion.
Reflection

Running an independent study on a live production game without studio access sharpened my ability to extract signal from purely observational methods. The most valuable outcome wasn't any single finding but the realization that cross-participant pattern recognition is where the real research judgment lives: four of five players independently flagging the same system is a pattern worth testing with more people.

Role
Co-Lead UX Researcher (volunteer, via Tech Fleet), Phase 2: usability guide, screener survey, and recruitment flow
Context
Six-person volunteer research team (2 leads, 4 apprentices) working with two UX design teams and UX writing on an open-source accessibility EdTech app
The project
An open-source dyslexia app for students in West Africa, built by Dyslexia Foundation Nigeria (DFN) with Tech Fleet volunteer teams. Earlier phases had defined the problem space and product goals. Phase 2 had to get the student prototype ready to test with children and their parents.
The question
Was the student prototype ready to put in front of children? Could they sign up, log in and find a game on their own, and what would parents need in order to agree?
My role
Co-Lead UX Researcher for Phase 2 (September to November 2024), one of 2 leads on a six-person research team with 4 apprentices, working alongside two UX design teams and UX writing.
What this can and cannot tell you
This is a preparation phase. My work was the guide, the screener and the recruitment plan. Usability testing with students was planned for the final sprint, and I do not have its results.

1The problem

The research team had no participant pool yet. Testing with children means parental consent, a confirmed testing location and confirmed device access, and all three were still open. The student persona carried over from Phase 1 and needed more research to be fully validated.

In scope: the student prototype first, then materials for educators. Out of scope: building the app itself.

2What I did

  • Usability testing guide. I drafted a hybrid guide that mixed interview questions with tasks (signing up, logging in, finding a game), written in kid-friendly language so children could follow it.
  • Screener survey. I refined the parent and student survey into a screener and added questions about technology access at home and at school, because devices were an open question.
  • Recruitment flow. I helped build a parent email, a consent form and a pre-test survey.
  • Persona check. I compared each student persona statement with earlier interviews, surveys, focus groups and desk research. It produced minor changes and a note that more research was needed.
  • Incentives. Offering payment was not an option, so I brainstormed alternatives such as workshops for parents and teachers through DFN or other experts.

3What got in the way

There was no ready participant pool. The team was still reaching out to other dyslexia organizations and online groups for participants, and the guide and survey were ready before the participants were.

4What happened next

The decks I have show testing materials for students under review with design and writing, and educator materials started, with usability testing planned for the final sprint. My phase ended in November 2024, and I do not have testing results to report.

5What I would do differently

Start recruitment in the first sprint. Parental consent, a testing location and device access took the longest to settle.

Reflection

Testing with children means consent, language, and device access have to be settled before the first session. That made the guide and the screener as important as the prototype itself.
