01Indie Games · Eldritch House · Nov 2025 – Present

Shadow Sacrament: The Roots of Evil · Early-Stage Indie Game Research

The developers wanted to know whether players were failing because the puzzles were hard or because the game was not telling them what to do.

Role
Embedded UX Researcher: moderated playtests, localization audit, and cross-session synthesis
Context
Embedded research partner to a Brazil-based indie studio

Ongoing embedded research on Shadow Sacrament, a survival horror indie game, now at 19 playtest sessions. Work spans moderated playtests routed by a performance-based participant framework, a frame-by-frame PT-BR localization audit, and same-day escalation of critical bugs to the dev team, including a keybind remapping issue that decoupled animation from function and PT-BR text bleeding into the English locale.

Moderated PlaytestingLocalization AuditParticipant Routing FrameworksSession ReportingBug EscalationIndie Games
What decision was the study meant to inform?
Whether players struggle because a puzzle is hard or because the game is not giving clear cues and feedback, and whether the map helps them find their place in the world. The developers approved a two-week plan built on four questions: do players understand what a puzzle wants, do they notice the feedback, can they use the map to know where they are and where to go, and does the map feel like part of the world.
What was in and out of scope?
In scope: puzzles, menus such as inventory and files, and the map, tested with players who match the game's audience: fans of Resident Evil style games in 2D. Out of scope: combat balance, boss tuning, difficulty settings, and story. Players raised those anyway. I recorded their comments and passed them on when the developers' per-act questions asked for them, but I did not study them. A survey was deferred until a wider demo, and the localization audit was not in the original plan.
What data did you collect and why?
Think-aloud playtests, because hesitation and misreading show up in what players say while they are stuck. Short interviews right after each session, while players still remember what confused them. The plan began with a heuristic review of the early build to pick which puzzles and map views to test. Recordings were reviewed frame by frame, which is how the pause menu cursor finding surfaced and how a 94-minute PT-BR playthrough (1,100+ frames) became 7 timestamped localization findings.
Why route players by non-boss death count?
Acts could only be started one at a time, so each player had to be sent to the act that fit them. Counting deaths outside boss fights gave a rough read on comfort with moment-to-moment play without scripted boss spikes skewing it, so puzzle and map clarity could be tested on players who were not fighting the game. The weakness: deaths can come from confusing feedback as well as low skill. One tester lost most of his runs to axe parry timing, and some enemies showed no stealth indicator.
What did you find about puzzles and feedback?
Players singled out the Carbine file puzzle and tap-to-enter safe codes as things that worked. In Acts 3 to 5, one tester skipped story content because doors and key items were not gated in that build, which he put down to its work-in-progress state. Feedback was the weaker side. The pause menu defaulted the cursor to Load Game, a destructive action, instead of Resume Game. Some enemies showed no stealth indicator, and players got little signal when they were spotted. A 12-minute gap separated picking up the lantern from learning how to activate it. In Act 3, an item used at the next spot produced no visual change, sound, or response, so players could not tell whether they had the right place or the right item.
What did you find about the map?
Act 3 sessions with two players showed navigation confusion, which the developers confirmed as a known issue: the Act 3 map did not show the Exit Door puzzle room. The current build now shows it with a [?] marker for unfinished business. A later tester found the map clear overall but harder to read in Acts 4 and 5, where fewer landmarks gave him less to orient by. I have the least evidence on the fourth question, whether the map feels like part of the world, so I do not claim an answer to it.
What happened next?
The developers fixed the Act 3 map gap, so the missing room now shows on the map with a [?] marker. The latest build's pause menu now starts on Resume Game. The developers could not say whether my report prompted that, but agreed that Load Game as the first option invites accidental presses. Three confirmed bugs went to the developers: PT-BR text in the English UI, keybind remapping that decoupled animation from door function, and reversed enemy audio spatialization. A crash I reported in Act 1 was acknowledged and is being fixed, and the developers now ask for the build version in every report so they know which iteration a finding refers to. On puzzle feedback, the developers called the missing response to an item used in Act 3 fair feedback. They also said the safe combination, statue, and lock puzzles are solvable from the in-game notes and have brute-force fallbacks so players cannot get locked out, which leaves the open question of whether players find and read those notes.
What would you do differently?
Two things. I would formalize the recurring-findings rule earlier: flag an issue once, and if the team does not respond, move it to an appendix so the priority signal stays clear across Acts. I would also pair death-count routing with a short self-reported experience question, so routing does not end up sorting players by how much the game confused them.
Reflection

Working with a small studio meant framing research as serving the game, before testing even began, and acknowledging design intent before describing where it broke down for players. That kept the relationship collaborative rather than adversarial, even when the news wasn't good.

02Self-Initiated Study · Clockwork Acorn · May – Oct 2026

Feather's Edge · New Player Controls Study

Nine of 11 players rated the controls clear, yet 7 said the jump did not behave as they expected. They knew which button did what. The movement itself surprised them.

Role
Independent researcher: study scoping, survey design, recruitment, analysis, and findings report
Context
Volunteer study I proposed to a small independent studio, run on the October 2026 demo of their upcoming platform game

In May 2026 I offered Clockwork Acorn volunteer research on Feather's Edge, a platform game whose movement and combat scheme differs from most platformers. After a call with the studio's head in September, we settled on a one-off study of how new players learn the controls in the public demo, against a single build, with participation capped and recruitment from my own channels.

Survey DesignNew Player OnboardingControls LearnabilitySelf-Directed RecruitmentStakeholder ReviewIndie Games
What decision was the study meant to inform?
Whether new players can work out an unusual movement and combat scheme without heavy tutorials. The studio's stated approach was to teach controls and goals and let players learn through play, and it had found the scheme worked less well for some enemies and for longer-term progression. The study asked what players learned by experimenting, what surprised them, and where they gave up.
What was in and out of scope?
In scope: the first-time experience of the October 2 demo build, covering the controls, movement, and combat rules. Out of scope: the input scheme itself, which the studio had locked, and any build other than the demo. The studio's own in-game survey already reached a wider audience, so mine was built to explain why patterns happen, not to measure how often.
What data did you collect and why?
A survey I designed, which the studio reviewed before launch and cleared as giving away nothing pre-release. Closed questions gave counts. Open questions, such as "What did you learn about fighting that the game never told you?", captured the rules players found on their own and the words they used for what surprised them.
How did you recruit, and what got in the way?
I posted the game's trailer in Reddit communities for indie games and Metroidvania-style games, and in some Discord channels. The studio's own community was small and had already been asked to complete the in-game survey, so we agreed I would source my own players, and I stopped engaging the studio's Discord when asked. The survey closed at 11 responses against a target of 15.
What did you find about movement?
Seven of 11 players said the jump did not behave as expected, though 9 of 11 rated the controls 4 or 5 for clarity in the first few minutes (mean 4.2). Only 4 said movement felt natural by the end of the first level. Five said later in the demo and 2 said never. Three players independently asked for a way to cancel a jump or release from a wall.
What did players learn without being told?
Four found on their own that dashing makes them immune to projectiles. Six described a rule tied to a single enemy type, and two players never worked out how to defeat one enemy and stopped playing there. Six said they knew why they took damage only some of the time, and nobody chose never.
Where did players stop, and what got in the way?
Two players finished the demo, five reported stopping early, and four left the question blank. Three of the five who stopped did so in the ice region. Three players also hit input problems on three different non-Xbox controllers. Only Xbox controllers are officially supported, so some of those may be known limits.
What happened next, and what would you do differently?
The findings report went to the studio on October 19. The studio head confirmed he had read it and said he would share it with the wider team. Next time I would ask every respondent the same questions: the question set changed between the first five responses and the last six, so several counts are out of 5 or 6 instead of 11. I would also recruit players new to platformers, since 9 of 11 knew the genre well and the study cannot speak to newcomers.
Reflection

A self-selected sample of 11 cannot say how common a problem is. It can show what the problems look like and what players say about them, which is why the report gives counts instead of percentages and lists its limits. The studio's larger in-game survey can show whether the jump, dash, and ice region patterns hold at scale.

03Independent Study · Disney Speedstorm · May 2024

Disney Speedstorm · Independent UX Research Study

New players couldn't tell what the in-game currencies were for, and it wasn't one confused player, it was the same gap in four sessions out of five.

Role
Independent researcher: study design, participant recruitment, facilitation, and synthesis
Context
Independent observational study conducted via PlaytestCloud credits received upon completing Steve Bromley's Games User Research Fundamentals course

An independent observational study on Disney Speedstorm, a live-service mobile racing game by Gameloft, conducted as a practical application of the Games User Research Fundamentals course. Five moderated think-aloud sessions across 4 countries, focused on new player onboarding, progression comprehension, and early-game friction.

Think-Aloud PlaytestingObservational ResearchCross-Session SynthesisBehavioral AnnotationMental Model AnalysisLive-Service GamesNew Player Onboarding
What decision was the study meant to inform?
No studio commissioned this study, so the question was mine, written as if for the game's product team: do new players understand what the in-game currencies and rewards are for, and does the early game teach them what to do next? Disney Speedstorm suited it because the brand has strong appeal but sits on a complex progression economy that new players meet from the first session.
What was in and out of scope?
In scope: new player onboarding, progression comprehension, and early-game friction. Out of scope: anything that needed studio access or internal data, such as analytics or balance numbers. With PlaytestCloud credits from the course and no studio access, I observed five new players and did not try to measure how common any problem is.
What methods did you use and why?
Moderated think-aloud sessions via PlaytestCloud let me observe real new-player behavior without studio access. Five sessions across a diverse participant pool (ages 17-36, 4 countries) gave enough signal to identify cross-participant patterns. I developed a behavioral annotation system using tags to structure cross-session synthesis.
What did you find?
The most consistent cross-participant finding was confusion around in-game currency and reward systems, flagged independently by 4 of 5 participants regardless of age or gaming experience. Onboarding clarity split clearly by player archetype: experienced kart-racing players found it sufficient; genre-unfamiliar players did not.
What would you do differently?
With more time I would have recruited specifically for Disney-primary players to better understand the experience gap for that audience, and designed a second evaluative round to test whether specific changes addressed the currency confusion finding.
What happened next?
The study produced a synthesis report with 7 prioritized findings and 5 design recommendations, linked below. No studio was involved, so the report is where the work ends, and I cannot say whether anything changed.
Reflection

Running an independent study on a live production game without studio access sharpened my ability to extract signal from purely observational methods. The most valuable outcome wasn't any single finding but the realization that cross-participant pattern recognition is where the real research judgment lives: one player struggling with currency is noise; four players independently flagging the same system is a finding worth acting on.

04Web3 / Trading Analytics · Kadena · Oct 2022 – May 2023

Kadena K-Block Explorer · Trader and NFT Research

Phase 1 left the design team with two personas and no research behind them. Phase 2 had eight weekly sprints to replace those guesses with interviews and usability data.

Role
Apprentice in Phase 1, then Co-Lead UX Researcher in Phase 2 (Mar to May 2023): one of 3 co-leads on an 8-person research team
Team Context
Volunteer research team via Tech Fleet, working alongside the design, content, and product strategy teams

Tech Fleet set out to build a second version of Kadena's blockchain explorer with three goals: an easy-mode interface, analytics for Kadena NFTs, and real-time trading tools. Phase 1 produced a competitive analysis, two exploratory interviews, and personas. The design team's first prototype was built around two personas, a rookie user and a returning user, without research behind them.

I joined Phase 1 as an apprentice and stepped up to co-lead in Phase 2. Over eight weekly sprints, from March 20 to May 12, 2023, the research team recruited participants, ran interviews and usability sessions, and passed findings to the design team in small batches while the work continued.

InterviewsModerated Usability TestingPersona UpdateCompetitive AnalysisBlockchain / Web3
What decision was the research meant to inform, and what was in scope?
Phase 1 left the team with two exploratory interviews and two personas that the design prototype had not been built from. The design team needed first-hand evidence from crypto and NFT users to update those personas, and usability data to revise the MVP. In scope: interviews with experienced crypto and NFT traders and current Kadena users, plus moderated usability tests of the Blockchain Explorer and the NFT Analytics Tool, using tasks the design team handed off. Out of scope: new-to-crypto users, who were not recruited until Phase 3. Eight weekly sprints and a starting goal of 5 interviews set the size of the study.
What methods did you use and why?
A competitive analysis of NFT analytics tools to find where the explorer could fill a gap. Nine interviews (6 participants experienced in crypto and NFT trading, 3 current Kadena users) to update the personas. Two rounds of moderated usability testing, first on the Blockchain Explorer and then on the NFT Analytics Tool, using tasks the design team handed off. Transcripts were coded in FigJam, and each interviewer and coder wrote a short proto-persona so the team could build personas together.
What did you find?
Interviews: participants preferred mainstream explorers like Etherscan to verify tokens and transactions, used tools like CoinGecko for price data, and relied on social media for information behind their decisions. Their reasons for using Kadena were smart contracts, low gas fees, and community visibility. Round 1 (Blockchain Explorer): all 3 users completed every task step except one, which 2 of 3 completed. The dropdown menu went unused and mining terms confused people, while the familiar layout drew praise. Round 2 (NFT Analytics Tool): the Single Ease Question score was 6.7 of 7, and finding the NFT calendar was the main struggle.
What got in the way?
Recruiting. Web3 users are wary of scams, and some people who shared their contact details later backed out. The team reached out to active Kadena users on Twitter and Discord and through friends and family, starting with a goal of 5 interviews. Tech Fleet's CXO advised that even one participant a week was valuable.
What was your part?
I presented the NFT analytics competitive analysis at the Sprint 1 kickoff, contributed usability task ideas ahead of the joint task session with the design team, and took part in participant outreach. As one of 3 co-leads, I shared responsibility for the team's deliverables at the May 2023 Final Sprint Demo.
What would you do differently?
I would separate interviews and usability testing by default. We combined them in one session, which sounds efficient but can be too much for a participant with a tight schedule. Separate sessions suit those participants, and a combined session fits someone with a wide-open schedule. The team's retrospective also found that the combined format cut into the depth of the interviews. I would also have every team send members to other teams' meetings, since that kept the projects aligned.
Reflection

I started this project doubting it. Several crypto companies closed while we worked, and researching a crypto product felt risky. Finding active communities across social media changed that: people were still busy with cryptocurrency, mining, and NFTs, and many were willing to talk with us and suggest improvements. Sitting in on other teams' meetings also paid off. Knowing what each team needed let us build parts of their tasks into our research, and it gave us direction when our own next steps were unclear. Completing two rounds of interviews and usability testing in eight weeks was a real accomplishment for the team.

What Happened Next

Later Tech Fleet teams carried the work through Phases 3 and 4. Phase 3 opened by reanalyzing the Phase 1 and 2 data to set its research goals. Its design team started from the Phase 2 prototype, ran usability tests on it, and rebuilt pages including the NFT dashboard, neighboring chains, and wallet overview. Phase 2 had reached mostly experienced users, and Phase 3 recruited Newbies alongside two experience levels. By the end of Phase 3 the design team had a beta-launch-ready responsive web app handed off to developers, with a live site shown at the September 2023 demo. The front-end beta is publicly hosted at kblock-explorer.vercel.app; it still shows placeholder data because Phase 3 recommended that the next phase focus on backend development. Phase 3 also ran its own usability study and added a Resources page with a glossary and tooltips to address the term confusion the research had been tracking since Phase 1. Phase 3's research team recommended updating personas and testing updated screens next, and Phase 4 interviewed two beginner groups.

05EdTech / Accessibility · DFN · Jul 2023 – Jan 2026

Dyslexia Foundation Nigeria · Accessibility Research

A distributed research team had no consistent way to track participants or sessions, and findings kept getting lost between phases.

Role
Lead UX Researcher (via Tech Fleet): infrastructure, mixed-methods research, and synthesis
Context
Distributed research team supporting an open-source accessibility EdTech app for dyslexic students across West Africa

Multi-phase research engagement for an open-source EdTech app built to support dyslexic students in West Africa. Work spanned three-plus research phases over two and a half years, including discovery, competitive benchmarking, and evaluative testing with trainers, caregivers, and educators, alongside building the participant tracking and documentation infrastructure a distributed team needed to stay consistent.

Accessibility ResearchEdTechMixed MethodsResearch InfrastructureLead Researcher
What decision was the research meant to inform, and what was in scope?
The product team needed to know who the app's users were and what they already used, how existing tools set the bar, and whether the app worked for the people around dyslexic students. Scope followed three phases, discovery, competitive benchmarking, and evaluative testing, with trainers, caregivers, and educators as the participant groups. Before any of that could matter, the team needed shared records, so building them came first.
What methods did you use and why?
A mixed-methods approach across discovery, competitive benchmarking, and evaluative testing, chosen to match a team working with limited resources and inconsistent connectivity. Device and connectivity data (97.3% Android usage) anchored technical recommendations the product team could act on directly.
What happened to the project?
After sustained progress across multiple research phases, communication with the project slowed and eventually stopped. I do not have full visibility into the cause. My best guess is a mix of general communication breakdown and a change in project leadership, where the incoming manager may not have had context on the work already completed.
What would you do differently?
Build a lightweight handoff document as the work progresses, something a new stakeholder could read in ten minutes to get oriented on what has been tested, decided, and left open. I would also establish more than one point of contact on the organization's side, so a single personnel change does not leave the research thread with no one to follow up with.
Reflection

Research infrastructure is not just documentation for its own sake. It is what makes the work portable when the people around it change, which matters most in nonprofit and distributed-team settings where turnover is common and institutional memory is thin. This project did not end the way I would have wanted, but it clarified what continuity actually requires.
